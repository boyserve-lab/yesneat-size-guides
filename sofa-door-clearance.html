Sofa width, depth and door clearance in inches

A sofa that does not fit through the door is a return that costs more than the sofa. The deciding number is usually depth, not width, and the constraint people forget is the floor behind the door.

A sofa is never carried flat through a doorway. It is stood on end, which makes depth the deciding dimension, or rotated through the corner, which makes width the deciding dimension and adds a floor-clearance requirement on both sides of the opening.

The door is not the width printed on it

A standard interior door slab is 32 inches wide and 80 inches tall. Once the jamb and hinge side are accounted for, the usable opening is 30 to 31 inches. Older homes and apartment stock run narrower: 28 inches is common, and 24 inches shows up on closets and bathroom doors. Measure the opening at its narrowest point, not the door slab.

Depths are measured with back cushions in place. Remove them for the doorway measurement: they add 4 to 6 inches of apparent depth and compress under load.

Two ways a sofa goes through

On end. The sofa stands vertically and its depth becomes the width being fed through the opening. A 38 inch deep sofa does not go through a 30 inch opening on end, no matter how narrow the sofa is. This is the case most people get wrong.

Rotated through the corner. The sofa enters at an angle, so width and depth both have to fit the diagonal of the opening plus the surrounding floor. The diagonal of a 31 by 80 inch opening is about 84 inches, which clears most sofas, but only when there is room to rotate.

The floor behind the door

A sofa standing on end needs about 42 inches of clear floor beyond the door on the entry side to swing through. A hallway 36 inches wide cannot supply that, so the sofa has to be lifted over the obstruction or brought in through a window. This is why the same sofa fits one apartment and not the one next door.

Elevators and stairwells

A 36 by 80 inch elevator door accepts a sofa flat only if the sofa's width fits the door height, which it usually does, and the elevator car is deep enough to angle it. Cars under 60 inches deep force the same on-end geometry as a hallway, with a heavier object and less room to maneuver. Stairwells add a second constraint: a 30 inch stairwell with a 7 foot ceiling clearance will not turn a 90 inch sofa at the landing, and no measurement of the door predicts that.

The measurement order that works

Measure the opening at the narrowest point. Measure sofa depth with the back cushions removed. Measure the clear floor on both sides of the door, and the depth of the elevator car if there is one. If the depth is under the opening width and there is 42 inches of floor, the sofa goes through; if either fails, it does not, and no amount of tilting changes that.
